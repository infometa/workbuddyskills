What is Corporate Insolvency under Vietnamese Law?
Corporate insolvency in Vietnam is defined as a situation where an enterprise is unable to meet its debt obligations when they become due.
The primary legislation governing corporate bankruptcy is the Law on Bankruptcy No. 51/2014/QH13, which came into effect on January 1, 2015.
It is important to distinguish between three related concepts:
Insolvency: A financial condition, not a legal status, indicating a company’s inability to pay debts.
Restructuring (business recovery): A legal process that allows a financially troubled company to recover operations.
Bankruptcy: A formal legal status declared by a court after determining the company is insolvent and recovery is not feasible.

The Corporate Bankruptcy Procedure in Vietnam
The bankruptcy process is initiated by filing a petition with the
People’s Court
in the locality where the business is headquartered. The procedure generally includes the following stages:
Court Reception
: The court will receive the petition, assign judges to handle petition and notify to the filers for modification or supplementation.
Court Acceptance
: If the petition meets legal requirements, the court issues a notice of acceptance, Fee and Bankruptcy expenses.
Appointment of Asset Manager (Trustee)
: An asset management officer or asset management and liquidation firm is assigned to manage the company’s assets.
Creditors’ Meetings:
Before declaring bankruptcy, the parties may discuss a restoration plan within 30 days after the creditors’ meeting approves a resolution on application of measures to restore business operation.
Declaration of Bankruptcy
: If the enterprise or cooperative fails to make a business operation restoration plan, the court issues an enforcement of decisions and auction of remaining assets.
The court plays a central role in supervising the process, including asset handling, creditor meetings convening, and approving recovery or liquidation plans.
Director & Management Responsibilities During Insolvency
When a company enters financial distress, directors and senior managers must exercise heightened legal care to avoid personal liability including: Individuals, agencies and organization and ensure full compliance with Vietnam’s bankruptcy regulations. The Law on Bankruptcy imposes specific fiduciary duties on management throughout the insolvency process.
Timely Disclosure:
Asset management officers are required to recognize when the company becomes insolvent and must not delay filing for bankruptcy if the legal conditions are met. Failing to act promptly may be interpreted as an attempt to conceal insolvency and could trigger legal consequences.
Preservation of Assets:
Once insolvency is apparent or bankruptcy proceedings are initiated, directors are prohibited from hiding, dispersing or donating their assets (Art 48). This duty helps protect creditor interests and prevents fraudulent dissipation of value.
Duty to Creditors:
Directors must act in the best interest of the collective creditor pool. This includes avoiding preferential treatment for selected creditors and refraining from transactions that unfairly disadvantage others.
Record-Keeping and Cooperation:
Company leadership is expected to maintain accurate financial and operational records. Full cooperation with asset management officers (trustees) and the People’s Court is essential throughout the proceedings.
Non-compliance with these obligations can result in being disciplined, administratively sanctioned or examined for penal liability (Art 129), depending on the nature and seriousness of their violations. Engaging experienced legal counsel early in the process can help management navigate compliance obligations, avoid missteps, and uphold their responsibilities with transparency.
What Happens to Creditors and Shareholders During Insolvency?
In the event of corporate insolvency, creditors are among the first stakeholders to take action, and their involvement significantly shapes the outcome of the process. Once the court accepts a bankruptcy case, creditors must submit their claims to the court-appointed asset manager within the designated timeframe. These claims are then reviewed, validated, and prioritized according to the order established in the Law on Bankruptcy.

Creditors in the highest priority classes are more likely to recover a portion of what they are owed, whereas lower-tier creditors often receive only partial repayments or, in some cases, none at all.
For shareholders, the outlook is more challenging. Because equity holders are considered residual claimants, they are only entitled to any remaining assets after all creditor claims have been satisfied. In most insolvency cases, especially those involving significant liabilities, there is little or no residual value left for shareholders. As a result, they often face a total loss of their investment.
Business Restructuring as an Alternative to Bankruptcy
Vietnamese law encourages business recovery solutions as a proactive measure to avoid formal insolvency proceedings. These options are particularly suitable for companies that are financially distressed but still fundamentally viable. Common restructuring strategies include (Art 88):
Raising capital
Reducing, writing off or rescheduling debts
Changing production and business lines
Renewing production technologies
Reorganizing the managerial apparatus; consolidating or splitting up production sections
Selling shares to creditors or others.
Selling or leasing assets.
Other lawful measures.
Under the Law on Bankruptcy, either the company or its creditors can submit a recovery plan to the court within 30 days after the creditors’ meeting approves a resolution on application of measures to restore business operation (Art 87). If the plan is deemed feasible and receives sufficient approval from the creditor committee and the court, the business may continue operations and gradually return to solvency.
This pathway is not only beneficial for the company but also protects the interests of creditors and employees by avoiding asset liquidation and business dissolution. For companies with strong underlying operations but temporary liquidity issues, restructuring can be a strategic and reputationally safer alternative to insolvency.
Cross-Border Insolvency Considerations
For multinational corporations and foreign creditors, engaging in insolvency matters in Vietnam involves a distinct set of legal and procedural complexities:
Participation of Foreign Creditors:
Foreigners are permitted to initiate and participate in insolvency proceedings, comply with Vietnam’s bankruptcy law.
Recognition of Arbitral Awards vs. Court Judgments:
Vietnam is a signatory to the New York Convention, which allows for the recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards. However, Vietnam does not formally recognize and enforce bankruptcy settlement decisions issued by foreign courts (Art 118), limiting the enforceability of court rulings from other jurisdictions.
Enforcement Challenges:
Pursuing cross-border claims often requires coordination with Vietnamese courts. Common obstacles include differences in legal systems, delays, and administrative procedures that can complicate asset recovery.
Legal and Strategic Planning:
Issues such as asset tracing, overlapping creditor claims, and regulatory inconsistencies make it essential to engage experienced local counsel early in the process. This is particularly important in cases involving subsidiaries, joint ventures, or complex cross-border ownership structures.
Given these complexities, foreign stakeholders should approach insolvency matters in Vietnam with a comprehensive strategy that accounts for both international and Vietnamese legal frameworks.
Common Challenges and Risks in Insolvency Proceedings
Despite having a legal framework in place, corporate insolvency in Vietnam is often hindered by:
Procedural delays due to court backlog
Inconsistent judicial interpretation
Limited coordination among creditors
Complexities in asset valuation and liquidation
Furthermore, bankruptcy carries significant reputational risks, particularly for companies with government ties or international investors. It is advisable for companies to evaluate restructuring and other viable solutions before entering into formal insolvency proceedings.
Conclusion
Corporate insolvency in Vietnam involves complex legal and operational considerations. While the Law on Bankruptcy provides a structured process, businesses should act early, assess recovery options, and understand the implications for management, creditors, and stakeholders.
