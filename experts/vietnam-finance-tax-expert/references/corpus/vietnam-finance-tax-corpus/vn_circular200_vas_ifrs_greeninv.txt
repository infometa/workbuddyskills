What are the differences between IFRS and VAS?
What are the differences between IFRS and VAS?
Writer: Nguyen Nguyet Anh at
06.10.2025
Blog
Vietnam is on the verge of a historic transformation in the field of finance and accounting. As the roadmap for adopting IFRS enters the mandatory phase for listed companies and large-scale FDI enterprises, a practical problem arises: Why do the same economic transactions yield completely different results when accounted for according to Vietnamese Accounting Standards (VAS) compared to International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)?
This difference lies not only in the techniques of recording debits and credits, but also stems from a core management mindset: one side relies on rigid rules, while the other relies on flexible principles. This creates significant "gaps" in profit figures and asset values, leaving many business owners and chief accountants confused when reading two parallel financial reports.
So, what exactly are the differences between IFRS and VAS? What are the key points that businesses need to consider when switching reporting formats? Let's analyze this in detail in the article below!
Differences in Conceptual Frameworks: "Principle" vs. "Rule" Thinking
The biggest difference, which serves as the "root" behind all the discrepancies in data between the two systems, lies in the philosophy behind standard development: VAS is based on Rules while IFRS is based on Principles.
Vietnamese Accounting Standards (VAS): Rule-Based Thinking
In Vietnam, accounting is not only a management tool but is also closely linked to the state's financial and tax management. Therefore, VAS (Vietnamese Accounting Standards) is highly "coercive" and formulaic.
Characteristics: Accountants perform their work based on detailed regulations from guiding circulars (typically Circular 200/2014/TT-BTC). Every transaction is pre-determined to be recorded in a specific account, with debit/credit entries as required.
Consequence: Accountants often focus on whether the documents are valid and legal according to tax regulations, rather than whether the transaction accurately reflects its economic nature. This creates a high degree of uniformity nationwide but lacks flexibility, sometimes resulting in financial reports that fail to reflect the diverse realities of business operations.
International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS): Principle-Based Thinking
Conversely, IFRS is designed to serve investors in the capital markets, and therefore emphasizes fairness and soundness.
Characteristics: IFRS does not provide rigid rules or detailed instructions for each accounting entry. Instead, it establishes core principles and a conceptual framework. To prepare reports, accountants and management are required to use their ability to assess, select, and make sound decisions.
Consequence: Regardless of the legal name of the contract, accountants must consider the true economic nature of the transaction in order to record it.
For example, a contract might be called a "Lease of Services" on paper, but if in essence the business controls the use of that asset for most of its economic lifecycle, IFRS requires it to be recognized as both an "Asset" and a "Lease Liability" on the balance sheet (according to IFRS 16), instead of just recognizing the lease expense as in VAS.
In summary: The transition from VAS to IFRS is a shift from a mindset of "following regulations precisely" to a mindset of "accurately reflecting business realities." This is the biggest human resources challenge for Vietnamese businesses.

The core difference: Original Price vs. Fair Value
If the difference in principles and rules is about mindset, then the difference in the basis for measuring asset value is what creates the biggest discrepancy in numbers on the balance sheet.
VAS: Upholding the Cost Principle
According to the Accounting Law and VAS 01 Standard, a company's assets are recorded at their original cost at the time of their creation (purchase price + related costs).
Mechanism: The book value of an asset remains virtually unchanged throughout its ownership period, except when depreciation or revaluation is applied in very special circumstances (such as the privatization of state-owned enterprises, splitting, or merging).
Limitation: While this principle ensures certainty and verifiability (with invoices and supporting documents), it makes financial reports "outdated" compared to reality.
A typical example: A company purchased an office building in District 1, Ho Chi Minh City 15 years ago for 20 billion VND. Currently, the market value of this building could reach 500 billion VND. However, according to the financial statements under VAS (Vietnam Asset Management), the building's value remains only 20 billion VND (after deducting accumulated depreciation). This obscures the company's true financial strength.
IFRS: Towards Fair Value
IFRS prioritizes the "relevance" of information to investor decision-making. Therefore, IFRS makes extensive use of the Fair Value basis (specifically defined in IFRS 13).
Mechanism: IFRS allows (and in many cases requires) businesses to revalue assets and liabilities at market prices at the time of reporting.
For investment properties (IAS 40): Businesses may choose to recognize them at fair value. The increase in value is recognized immediately in the Income Statement, increasing profit for the period.
For Financial Instruments (IFRS 9): Investments in stocks and bonds must be valued at market price.
Impact: When transitioning to IFRS, the asset size and equity of many Vietnamese businesses (especially in the real estate and banking sectors) often surge due to asset revaluation closer to reality. However, this also brings volatility risk: If the market declines, businesses are forced to record losses immediately.
This difference explains why, for the same business, financial statements prepared according to IFRS are often more attractive to foreign investors because they accurately reflect the actual financial position.
Differences in Accounting Account Systems
This difference stems from management objectives: one side prioritizes standardization to facilitate inspections by government agencies (VAS), while the other prioritizes flexibility to serve the management needs of the enterprise (IFRS).
VAS: Unified and Compulsory Accounting System
A prominent characteristic of Vietnamese accounting is its standardized system of accounting codes. The Ministry of Finance (through Circular 200/2014/TT-BTC) issued a unified list of accounting accounts applicable to all businesses in the economy (except for some specific sectors such as banking and insurance, which have their own systems).
Strict regulations: Businesses are required to use the correct account numbers (e.g., Cash is 111, Accounts Receivable is 131, Revenue is 511...). Modifications or additions to level 1 accounts usually require approval from the Ministry of Finance.
Purpose: To facilitate tax authorities and regulatory agencies in easily verifying, cross-referencing data, and compiling national statistics. However, this limits the customization capabilities of diversified corporations with complex operational structures.
IFRS: No model account system prescribed.
Conversely, IFRS standards (specifically IAS 1 - Presentation of Financial Statements) only prescribe the content, structure, and minimum items that must be presented in financial statements, but do not prescribe the internal accounting system.
Flexibility: Businesses have full discretion to design the accounting system that best suits their specific business characteristics and management requirements. There is no mandatory rule that "Cash" must be code 111; it can be code 1000 or A01 depending on the business's coding style.
Transition Challenge: When adopting IFRS, Vietnamese businesses often face significant difficulties regarding technical infrastructure. Most domestic accounting software is "hardcoded" according to the VAS chart of accounts. To adopt IFRS, businesses are required to perform mapping – that is, comparing and converting the chart of accounts from VAS to IFRS – or upgrade their ERP system to be able to operate multiple sets of accounting books in parallel.
Differences in Information Presentation and Disclosure Requirements
While VAS focuses on "recording data," IFRS focuses on "interpreting the meaning of the data." For international investors, the Notes to Financial Statements under IFRS are just as important, if not more important, than the numbers on the Balance Sheet.
VAS: Disclosure of information at a basic level
According to VAS 21 and Circular 200, Vietnam's explanatory requirements mainly involve listing and providing detailed explanations for the figures presented.
Characteristics: The explanatory notes typically focus on interpreting the balances: What does inventory consist of? Who are the receivables from? How have fixed assets increased or decreased?
Limitations: There is little mention of non-financial information, management assumptions, or future risk analyses. Readers only know "what the result was" but struggle to understand "why that result was reached" and "what the potential risks are."
IFRS: Requirements for Transparency and In-depth Risk Analysis
IFRS (specifically IAS 1 and related standards such as IFRS 7 and IFRS 13) sets stringent requirements for information disclosure to protect investors. Reports should not only present past figures but also provide insightful perspectives on asset quality and risk management capabilities.
The three biggest differences in IFRS disclosures include:
Announcing the key Assumptions and Judgments:
The company must clearly explain why this depreciation method was chosen. Why is the estimated useful life 10 years instead of 20 years? Why is this debt assessed as recoverable?
Risk Management and Sensitivity Analysis:
This is a mandatory requirement for financial instruments (according to IFRS 7). Businesses must answer the "What-if" question: If interest rates increase by 1%, by how much will business profits decrease? If the exchange rate fluctuates by 5%, how will debt increase? VAS does not require this at all.
Fair Value Hierarchy:
When recording assets at market value, businesses must clearly disclose the source of their data: from listed assets (Level 1), from similar assets (Level 2), or from their own calculations using models (Level 3). This prevents businesses from inflating asset values ​​without justification.
IFRS-compliant reporting provides a more comprehensive, multifaceted, and truthful picture of a company's health, helping investors accurately assess risks before making investment decisions.
