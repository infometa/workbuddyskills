Vietnam Investment Law 2026: Practical Checklist to Qualify for Investment Incentives
By Global Law Experts
– posted 2 months ago
Legal
Vietnam
Foreign Investment
Vietnam’s amended Investment Law, effective 1 March 2026, overhauls the way foreign investors qualify for vietnam investment incentives, replacing a broad, sector-wide regime with a targeted framework that ties benefits directly to project characteristics such as technology intensity, capital scale, and geographic location.  For general counsel, CFOs, and investment managers structuring inbound transactions, the reform creates both opportunity and urgency: projects that meet the new eligibility tests can access preferential corporate income tax (CIT) rates, import duty exemptions, and land-rent reductions, while those that fail risk losing incentives previously taken for granted.
This guide provides a practitioner-level eligibility checklist, documents every procedural step from investment registration certificate (IRC) application through to post-approval inspection (hậu kiểm), and walks through four worked examples covering M&A, industrial parks, real estate, and R&D projects.  It is current as of 14 May 2026 and should be re-checked whenever new implementing decrees or Ministry of Planning and Investment (MPI) circulars are issued.
Quick Summary, What Changed in the Investment Law 2026 and Why It Matters for Vietnam Investment Incentives
The National Assembly passed the amended Investment Law in late 2025, with the core provisions taking effect on 1 March 2026. Implementing guidance followed through Decree 239/2025, which refines incentive-area definitions and introduces digital technology zone classifications, and Decree 96/2026, which provides detailed regulations and guidance on several articles of the amended law. Together, these instruments replace the older, largely automatic incentive regime with a framework that requires investors to demonstrate compliance with specific eligibility criteria before benefits are confirmed.
The reform also streamlines the list of conditional business activities in Vietnam. A number of licence-type conditions that previously created compliance bottlenecks, particularly in logistics, certain retail sub-sectors, and non-critical educational services, have been removed or reclassified. Conversely, new conditions have been added for activities involving critical data infrastructure and certain digital platforms, reflecting the government’s evolving regulatory priorities.
The transitional provisions of the investment law deserve close attention. Projects approved before 1 March 2026 generally retain their existing incentives for the remainder of the originally approved term, but any material change (expansion, ownership transfer, or scope amendment) can trigger re-assessment under the new framework. The practical consequence is that even “grandfathered” projects need to plan carefully before executing structural changes.
What this means for investors:
Incentives are no longer automatic.
Investors must pass defined eligibility tests, sector, scale, location, and technology, and document compliance at the application stage.
Conditional activities have shifted.
Review your business lines against the updated list to confirm whether a new or amended sub-licence is required.
Existing projects are not immune.
Any structural change to a pre-March 2026 project may activate the new rules, potentially altering the incentive package.
Types of Vietnam Investment Incentives Available Under the 2026 Framework
The 2026 framework retains the core categories of incentive that have historically attracted FDI into Vietnam, but it refines the qualifying conditions and, in several cases, adjusts the duration or magnitude of benefits. Industry observers expect these refinements to channel investment more effectively toward high-value-add sectors and underserved regions, rather than subsidising projects that would proceed regardless.
Preferential CIT rates remain a centrepiece. Qualifying projects may access a rate of 10% (versus the standard 20%) for a defined period, often 15 years, with tax holidays of up to four years and a 50% reduction for up to nine subsequent years for projects in especially encouraged sectors or locations. Import duty exemptions continue to apply to fixed assets, raw materials, and components that are not yet produced domestically, provided the goods are used directly in the incentivised project. Land rent exemptions or reductions, ranging from full exemption for the first 11–15 years to partial reductions thereafter, are available for projects located in encouraged areas, including economic zones, industrial parks, and newly designated digital technology zones under Decree 239/2025.
Non-fiscal incentives have also gained prominence. These include fast-track administrative processing for IRC applications, dedicated infrastructure support commitments from provincial authorities, and, for projects meeting national strategic thresholds, access to state investment credit and export credit facilities.

How to Get Vietnam Investment Incentives: The Incentive Eligibility Checklist for FDI
Qualifying for incentives under the investment law 2026 Vietnam framework requires passing a series of cumulative tests. Failure on any single test does not necessarily disqualify a project, some incentive categories require only a subset, but the strongest packages are reserved for projects that satisfy all relevant criteria. The checklist below is designed as a pass/fail diagnostic that investment teams can apply during the feasibility stage.
Sector Test
The starting point is whether the proposed investment activity falls within an encouraged sector. Under the Investment Law 2026, encouraged sectors are defined in the law itself and elaborated in Decree 239/2025, which introduced updated sector lists including new digital technology zone classifications. Key encouraged sectors include:
High technology and supporting industries
, semiconductors, advanced electronics, precision engineering, and designated supporting industries for global supply chains.
Research and development
, projects allocating a prescribed minimum percentage of revenue to R&D activities, or establishing dedicated R&D facilities.
Environmental protection and clean energy
, waste treatment, renewable energy generation, and carbon reduction technology.
Education, healthcare, and culture
, with specific sub-sector conditions (note that some education activities have been reclassified as conditional).
Agriculture and rural development
, high-tech agriculture, aquaculture, and food processing in designated regions.
Infrastructure development
, industrial parks, economic zones, and digital technology zones designated under Decree 239/2025.
Investors should cross-reference their specific activity code against the updated Vietnamese Industry Classification System (VSIC) codes referenced in the decrees. The Vietnam Embassy also publishes a summary list of sectors entitled to investment incentives, which serves as a useful starting reference.
Scale and Capital Expenditure Test
Certain incentive tiers are reserved for projects meeting minimum investment capital thresholds. While the precise thresholds vary by sector and location, the general pattern under the 2026 framework requires:
Standard encouraged projects:
Minimum registered capital as specified in the IRC; no universal floor, but provincial authorities may apply de facto minimum expectations.
Large-scale projects qualifying for enhanced incentives:
Minimum total investment capital of VND 6,000 billion (approximately USD 240 million) or meeting sector-specific thresholds for national strategic status.
Digital technology zone projects:
Must meet the technology-intensity criteria set out in Decree 239/2025, which emphasise R&D spending ratios and workforce qualification levels over raw capital amounts.
For projects near the threshold boundary, early engagement with provincial or MPI officials is advisable to confirm that the capital structure as documented in the feasibility study and IRC application will satisfy the applicable test.
Location and Planning
Location is the second major determinant. The Investment Law 2026 retains the geographic classification system, areas with extremely difficult socio-economic conditions, areas with difficult socio-economic conditions, and standard areas, but adds digital technology zones and innovation districts as new qualifying locations. Projects sited within designated industrial parks, export-processing zones, or economic zones continue to receive automatic location-based incentives, subject to the other eligibility tests. For investors evaluating site selection, Decree 239/2025 provides updated lists of encouraged locations by province. Provincial People’s Committees may also designate additional incentive sub-zones, so checking both national and local lists is essential.
Investors undertaking
real estate transactions
in Vietnam should note that the location test interacts with land-use planning approvals, a project may meet the sector test but fail to qualify for land-rent incentives if the land use is inconsistent with the provincial master plan.
Non-Licence Compliance: Environment, Land, and Labour
Even projects that pass the sector, scale, and location tests must demonstrate compliance with non-licence conditions to secure the full incentive package:
Environmental impact assessment (EIA):
Required for all projects above prescribed thresholds; the EIA report must be approved before the IRC is issued.
Land-use rights:
The investor must demonstrate a lawful basis for land access, lease, sub-lease from an industrial park developer, or allocation from the provincial authority, and the intended use must be consistent with the land-use planning certificate.
Labour and training commitments:
Projects claiming enhanced incentives (especially in encouraged areas) may be required to commit to hiring local workers and providing vocational training, with KPIs that are monitored during post-inspection (
hậu kiểm
).
Fire safety and construction permits:
Must be obtained before commercial operations commence; delays here can affect the incentive commencement date.
Investors requiring
consular legalisation of foreign documents for use in Vietnam
should factor legalisation timelines into their overall project schedule, as foreign-origin documents used in the IRC application typically require authentication.
Application Process and Documentation for the Investment Registration Certificate in Vietnam
Securing vietnam investment incentives is a multi-step administrative process. The flowchart below outlines the typical pathway from company formation through to the incentive decision.
Documents for IRC and Incentive Proposals
The core application package for an IRC under the Investment Law 2026 includes:
Investment project proposal
, covering the investor’s identity, proposed activities (with VSIC codes), total investment capital, implementation schedule, and requested incentives with supporting justification.
Feasibility study
, including market analysis, financial projections, technology description, and environmental and social impact assessment summaries.
Enterprise Registration Certificate (ERC)
, the investor must typically establish a Vietnamese legal entity or branch before or concurrently with the IRC application.
Financial capacity evidence
, bank statements, audited financial reports of the foreign parent, or a combination demonstrating the ability to fund the registered capital.
Land-use documentation
, preliminary lease agreement, confirmation from the industrial park management board, or provincial land allocation decision.
Environmental impact assessment report
, approved or submitted to the relevant authority (depending on project scale).
Legalised and notarised copies of the investor’s corporate documents
, charter, board resolutions authorising the investment, and power of attorney for the local representative.
For projects involving conditional business activities in Vietnam, the investor must also submit sector-specific sub-licence applications (for example, a conditional licence for data centre operations or a retail distribution licence) either concurrently with or following the IRC application, depending on the sector.
Who Signs and Approves: MPI Versus Provincial People’s Committee
The approval authority depends on the project’s scale, sector, and location:
Provincial People’s Committee (PPC):
The default approval authority for most FDI projects. The PPC’s Department of Planning and Investment (DPI) receives the application, conducts the review, and issues the IRC. The PPC also confirms sector-based incentive eligibility.
Ministry of Planning and Investment (MPI):
Exercises approval authority over projects requiring National Assembly or Prime Ministerial investment policy approval, typically projects exceeding VND 10,000 billion in capital, involving casino or aviation activities, or located in sensitive areas.
Industrial park / economic zone management boards:
For projects located within designated zones, the management board often acts as a one-stop-shop, receiving the application and coordinating with the PPC.
Timelines and Practical Tips
Statutory processing timelines under the Investment Law 2026 are as follows, though actual timelines frequently exceed statutory limits due to inter-agency consultation requirements:
IRC issuance (non-conditional sectors):
15 working days from receipt of a complete application (statutory); 30–90 calendar days in practice.
IRC issuance (conditional sectors):
Additional time for sub-licence review; 45–120 calendar days in practice depending on sector complexity.
Investment policy approval (national-level):
45–90 working days (statutory) for projects requiring Prime Ministerial approval; substantially longer for National Assembly-level approval.
Incentive decision confirmation:
Typically issued concurrently with or shortly after the IRC, but may require a separate confirmation letter from the provincial tax authority for CIT incentives.
Practical tips: submit a complete application to avoid re-filing; engage the DPI or management board informally before formal submission to pre-clear eligibility questions; and ensure all foreign documents are legalised well in advance. Investors entering Vietnam on a
Vietnam business visa
should coordinate their visa timeline with the application schedule to ensure key signatories are available in-country when needed.
Transitional Provisions and Post-Inspection (
Hậu Kiểm
) for Existing Projects
The transitional provisions of the investment law are critical for the hundreds of existing FDI projects approved before 1 March 2026. Understanding these rules is essential to preserving existing incentive packages and avoiding inadvertent re-assessment.
Projects Approved Before 1 March 2026, Options and Risks
The general principle, as established in the Investment Law 2026 and further detailed in Decree 96/2026, is that incentives granted under previously valid investment certificates or IRCs continue to apply for the remainder of the originally approved term. However, several triggers can cause a project to be re-assessed under the new rules:
Material scope change:
Adding new business lines, significantly expanding production capacity, or changing the project’s location triggers a re-application and potential incentive re-assessment.
Ownership transfer or M&A:
A change of investor, whether through share transfer, merger, or asset sale, requires notification to the licensing authority and may trigger
hậu kiểm
.
Failure to meet implementation milestones:
If a project has not deployed the registered capital or commenced operations within the timeline stated in the IRC, the licensing authority may revoke or adjust incentives.
Voluntary amendment request:
Investors seeking to upgrade their incentive package under the new (potentially more favourable) rules may apply for amendment, but this subjects the entire project to the new eligibility tests.
Industry observers expect that many existing investors with projects in encouraged sectors will consider voluntary re-application to access enhanced incentives (particularly the new digital technology zone benefits), but the risk of losing existing entitlements during the review period must be carefully weighed.
Typical
Hậu Kiểm
Triggers and Corrective Steps
Post-inspection (
hậu kiểm
) in Vietnam refers to the periodic review by licensing authorities of whether a project continues to comply with the conditions under which its incentives were granted. Common triggers include:
Annual or periodic reviews
initiated by the provincial DPI or tax authority.
Complaint-driven inspections
, labour disputes, environmental incidents, or reports from competing businesses.
Post-M&A verification
, authorities confirming that the new investor meets the original eligibility conditions.
To prepare for
hậu kiểm
, companies should maintain a compliance file that includes the original IRC, all amendment decisions, employment records demonstrating local hiring and training KPIs, tax filings confirming incentive utilisation, and environmental monitoring reports. Where gaps are identified, proactive remediation and voluntary disclosure to the licensing authority are preferable to waiting for an adverse inspection finding.
Structuring Traps and Worked Examples: Preserving Vietnam Investment Incentives
Structuring errors are among the most common reasons foreign investors lose or fail to qualify for incentives. The following four worked examples illustrate typical scenarios and practical solutions.
Example 1: M&A of a Project Company With Existing Incentives
Fact pattern:
A foreign investor acquires 100% of the shares in a Vietnamese manufacturing company that holds an IRC with a 10% CIT rate for 12 remaining years. The acquirer plans no operational changes.
Applicable rule:
Under the Investment Law 2026, a transfer of ownership requires notification to and, in certain cases, approval from the licensing authority. The incentive package is tied to the project and the IRC, not to the identity of the investor, but authorities will verify that the new investor meets any conditions originally attached (e.g., minimum capital, technology commitments).
Practical solution:
File the ownership-transfer notification promptly. Include evidence that the acquirer satisfies the original eligibility conditions. Avoid altering the project scope or business lines during the transfer process, as any simultaneous scope change will trigger a full re-assessment under the 2026 rules. Retain the original management team through the transition period to demonstrate operational continuity.
Example 2: Corporate Restructure and Asset Purchase
Fact pattern:
Instead of acquiring shares, a foreign investor proposes to purchase the key operating assets (factory, equipment, land-use rights) of a Vietnamese project company and operate them through a newly established entity.
Applicable rule:
An asset purchase does not transfer the IRC or the associated incentives. The new entity must apply for a fresh IRC and satisfy the eligibility tests independently. The likely practical effect will be that the new entity can claim incentives only if it independently qualifies under the 2026 framework, which may or may not replicate the original package.
Practical solution:
Where preserving the existing incentive package is critical, a share acquisition is almost always preferable to an asset deal. If an asset structure is commercially necessary (for example, due to undisclosed liabilities), the investor should model the incentive impact of a fresh application before proceeding, and negotiate the purchase price to reflect any incentive shortfall.
Example 3: Industrial Park Developer Versus Tenant
Fact pattern:
A foreign-invested industrial park developer obtains an IRC with land-rent exemption for 15 years and CIT incentives. A tenant manufacturing company leases a plot within the park and assumes it will automatically receive the same incentives.
Applicable rule:
Incentives granted to the developer relate to the infrastructure development project. Tenants must apply separately for their own IRCs and demonstrate eligibility based on their specific activities, scale, and sector. However, the tenant benefits automatically from the location test (being within a designated zone) and may enjoy streamlined processing through the park’s management board.
Practical solution:
Tenants should not rely on the developer’s incentive package. Instead, they should file a standalone IRC application with the management board, clearly documenting how their project meets the sector and scale tests. The developer can assist by providing confirmation of the zone’s designation and available infrastructure, but the incentive decision is project-specific. Regulatory guidance similar in procedural nature to
drug registration in Vietnam
demonstrates that sector-specific approvals in Vietnam consistently require standalone applications even when umbrella approvals exist.
Example 4: Large-Scale Real Estate Versus Investment Project Threshold
Fact pattern:
A foreign investor plans a mixed-use urban development with total capital of VND 5,500 billion. The project includes residential, commercial, and hotel components. The investor expects investment incentives based on the project’s scale and the provincial government’s encouragement.
Applicable rule:
Under the 2026 framework, large-scale real estate projects qualify for certain incentives (particularly land-rent reduction and infrastructure support) only if they meet the capital threshold for enhanced incentive status and are located in an encouraged area. A project at VND 5,500 billion falls below the VND 6,000 billion threshold for national strategic treatment. Moreover, the residential component may not qualify for CIT incentives, as standard residential development is generally excluded from encouraged-sector lists.
Practical solution:
Consider restructuring the project to separate the hotel and commercial components (which may independently qualify under tourism or infrastructure incentive categories) from the residential component. Alternatively, if increasing registered capital to VND 6,000 billion is commercially feasible, this would cross the threshold, but the capital commitment must be genuine and demonstrable, as
hậu kiểm
will verify actual deployment.

How to Handle Refusals, Amendments, and Appeals
If an IRC application or incentive request is refused, or if a post-inspection results in an adverse finding, the investor has several practical avenues:
Administrative review:
Request the licensing authority (DPI or management board) to reconsider, providing supplementary documentation or clarification. This is the fastest and most common remedy.
Amendment application:
If the refusal relates to a specific condition (e.g., insufficient capital or missing environmental approval), remedy the deficiency and re-file. The Investment Law 2026 explicitly permits amendment applications.
Escalation to MPI:
For projects where the provincial authority’s decision appears inconsistent with national policy, the investor may petition MPI for guidance. MPI’s interpretation is not binding on the PPC but carries significant persuasive weight.
Administrative complaint or litigation:
Under the Law on Complaints and the Law on Administrative Procedures, the investor may file a formal complaint with the head of the licensing authority or initiate administrative litigation. In practice, litigation against licensing authorities is rare and is generally a last resort.
Investor-State arbitration:
For investors protected under bilateral investment treaties (BITs) or free trade agreements (such as CPTPP or EVFTA), treaty-based claims remain available for measures amounting to expropriation or denial of fair and equitable treatment, though such claims relate to the treatment of the investment rather than routine licensing decisions.
In all cases, maintaining comprehensive documentation from the application stage through to the refusal is essential. An immediate remediation checklist should include: (1) obtain the written refusal with stated reasons; (2) assess whether the deficiency is curable; (3) engage the licensing authority informally to understand the practical concern; and (4) re-file or escalate within the statutory timeframes.
Conclusion, Securing Vietnam Investment Incentives in 2026
The Investment Law 2026 represents a fundamental shift in how vietnam investment incentives are allocated.  The era of broad, largely automatic benefits has given way to a targeted framework that rewards projects demonstrating genuine alignment with Vietnam’s development priorities, technology intensity, strategic location, environmental sustainability, and workforce development.  For foreign investors, the practical imperative is clear: treat incentive eligibility as a structuring question from the earliest stage of project planning, not as an afterthought.  Use the eligibility checklist in this guide to diagnose qualification, assemble a complete application package, and plan for ongoing compliance and hậu kiểm.
Where structuring choices, particularly M&A versus asset purchase, or industrial park tenancy versus standalone project, will determine the incentive outcome, model the alternatives before committing.  This article is current as of 14 May 2026; readers should monitor MPI and provincial DPI publications for new implementing circulars and update their compliance approach accordingly.

FAQs
Which projects or activities are eligible for investment incentives under the Investment Law 2026?
Projects in encouraged sectors (high technology, R&D, clean energy, infrastructure, agriculture, and others), located in encouraged areas (industrial parks, economic zones, digital technology zones, areas with difficult socio-economic conditions), or meeting prescribed scale and technology thresholds are eligible. Eligibility requires passing the sector, scale, location, and compliance tests outlined in the checklist above, as set out in the Investment Law 2026 and Decree 239/2025.
Which conditional business activities were removed or reclassified under the 2026 amendments?
The 2026 amendments removed or reclassified a number of licence-type conditional activities, particularly in logistics, certain retail sub-sectors, and non-critical educational services. At the same time, new conditions were introduced for activities involving critical data infrastructure and certain digital platforms. Investors should review the updated conditional business list published by MPI and cross-reference with the sector-specific guidance in Decree 96/2026.
What transitional provisions apply to projects approved before 1 March 2026?
Projects approved before 1 March 2026 generally retain their existing incentive packages for the remainder of the originally approved term. However, material changes, including scope expansion, ownership transfer, or relocation, can trigger re-assessment under the new framework. Investors considering voluntary re-application to access enhanced 2026 incentives should weigh the risk of losing existing entitlements during the review period.
What documents and procedural steps are required to obtain an investment registration certificate and incentives?
The standard application package includes: investment project proposal, feasibility study, Enterprise Registration Certificate (ERC), financial capacity evidence, land-use documentation, environmental impact assessment, and legalised corporate documents. Applications are filed with the provincial DPI, industrial park management board, or MPI depending on the project’s scale and sector. Processing takes 30–120 calendar days in practice.
How does an M&A transaction affect existing incentive eligibility?
Share acquisitions generally preserve the existing IRC and associated incentives, provided the new investor meets the original eligibility conditions and files the required ownership-transfer notification. Asset purchases, by contrast, do not transfer the IRC, the new entity must apply independently. Any simultaneous change to the project scope during an M&A transaction will trigger a full re-assessment under the 2026 rules.
How long does it usually take to obtain an incentive decision?
Statutory processing times are 15 working days for non-conditional projects, but actual timelines range from 30 to 120 calendar days depending on project complexity, whether conditional sector sub-licences are required, and whether provincial or national-level approval is necessary. Early engagement with the licensing authority and submission of a complete application are the most effective ways to minimise delays.
What happens during post-inspection (hậu kiểm) and how should companies prepare?
Post-inspection in Vietnam involves a review by the licensing authority (typically the DPI or provincial tax authority) of whether the project continues to comply with the conditions under which incentives were granted. Companies should maintain a comprehensive compliance file, including the original IRC, all amendment decisions, employment records, tax filings, and environmental monitoring reports. Proactive remediation of any identified gaps before inspection is strongly advisable.
Can an investor voluntarily re-apply under the 2026 framework to upgrade their incentive package?
Yes. The Investment Law 2026 permits investors with pre-existing approvals to apply for amendment to access the new incentive framework. However, this subjects the entire project to the 2026 eligibility tests, which may result in a different, and not necessarily more favourable, incentive package. A cost-benefit analysis should be conducted before proceeding.
