HIGHLIGHTS • T he Egyptian economy has rebounded in 2025 after a period of
significant stress. Real gross domestic product (GDP) growth has
gathered pace, foreign-currency shortages have been resolved
and the inflation rate has halved due to a combination of base
effects, tight monetary policy and easing global price pressures.
• F iscal and monetary reforms have improved confidence in the
economy. Exchange-rate flexibility and fewer foreign-currency
restrictions have facilitated a return of foreign investment and
a recovery in domestic investment, while tax reforms and fiscal
consolidation have led to higher receipts and a fall in overall
debt levels.
• S tructural reforms have lagged macroeconomic stabilisation.
While the macroeconomy has recovered, progress on reforms
pertaining to the role of the state in the economy have largely
stalled over the past year. In particular, only modest progress has
been made on advancing the divestment programme and state
ownership policy.

KEY PRIORITIES • The government should persist with fiscal consolidation to improve debt
FOR 2026 sustainability. Although interest payments will continue to absorb a large part
of the budget, efforts to improve the fiscal balance by improving tax collection
and containing current expenditure would support economic resilience, while
the gradual reduction of debt levels would further boost investor confidence.
• Energy sector reform would improve the economy’s resilience while crowding
in private investment. Growth-enabling public investments, such as electricity
grid improvements, should be prioritised to enable greater investment in
renewable energy and lower Egypt’s reliance on energy imports.
• Reform of state-owned enterprises (SOEs) is critical to improve Egypt’s
competitiveness. Corporate and sectoral governance reforms to ensure a level
playing field and a reduced civilian and military state presence in the economy
would help shift Egypt towards a private-sector-driven growth model.

DEVELOPMENTS by 4.4 per cent year on year in fiscal year (FY) 2025 (from July 2024 to June 2025),
AND POLICY up from 2.4 per cent the preceding year. Growth was driven by the manufacturing
RESPONSE sector, which had contracted significantly during the exchange-rate crisis of
2023-24, followed by the tourism, and wholesale and retail trade sectors. The
oil and gas sector continued its long decline, however, with output shrinking in
12 of the prior 13 quarters.
Inflation is trending steadily downwards. In January to July 2025, consumer prices
rose by an average 15.7 per cent year on year, half the rate of the same period
the preceding year. Easing food prices, a tight monetary stance and base effects
contributed to the decline. Since April 2025 the Central Bank of Egypt has loosened
policy, cutting its main policy rate by a cumulative 625 basis points to 21.5 per cent.
The Egyptian pound traded within a narrow band against the US dollar in early 2025
before appreciating by more than 6.0 per cent since April, following an improvement
in foreign investor sentiment and overall dollar weakness.
The external accounts have seen a sustained recovery, but debt levels remain
burdensome. Remittances rose 82.7 per cent between July 2024 and March 2025,
while foreign investors returned to the short-term government debt market, owning
44.7 per cent of outstanding T-bills as at March 2025. Net international reserves

The economy is expected to improve further in 2025 and 2026 following
macroeconomic adjustment. Output growth is forecast to rise to 4.8 per cent in
2025 and 4.5 per cent in 2026 as business sentiment improves, private investment
recovers and access to external financing is restored. Downside risks include policy
backsliding, particularly on fiscal consolidation and structural reform, as well as
external shocks that may raise financing costs.

STRUCTURAL Egypt’s progress under its Extended Fund Facility (EFF) with the International
REFORM Monetary Fund (IMF) has been mixed. The fourth review of the IMF programme
DEVELOPMENTS was completed in March 2025 after some delays, with several structural benchmarks
not progressing as planned, including those on SOE reporting, competition law
amendments and an assessment of state-owned bank policies. Completion of the
review enabled the disbursement of US$ 1.2 billion (€1.1 billion), bringing total
disbursements under the EFF to US$ 3.2 billion (€2.8 billion). The programme was
enlarged from US$ 3.0 billion (€2.6 billion) to US$ 8.0 billion (€7.0 billion) in March
2024. The fifth EFF review was rescheduled to November 2025, to be combined with
the sixth review.

The government has successfully maintained a floating exchange rate. The
policy shift in March 2024 unified exchange rates and cleared corporate foreign-
currency demand backlogs at domestic banks, which have also gradually raised
foreign-exchange limits for retail customers. The parallel market has been largely
inactive since the policy shift. The government is working through arrears accumulated
by the Egyptian General Petroleum Corporation to international oil companies, one of
the final unresolved issues of the foreign-currency shortage period.
The government is undertaking reforms to advance fiscal consolidation.
The Ministry of Finance’s tax package aims to increase tax collections as a
percentage of GDP and widen the tax base. It includes 20 measures, including
eliminating exemptions, reforming the value-added tax system and modernising
the tax administration. To contain spending, the government plans to reduce
fuel subsidies with the aim of reaching cost recovery by the end of 2025. A cap
on public investment was also included in the FY2026 budget, similar to the
preceding fiscal year. The Ministry of Finance is improving its fiscal planning by
incorporating medium-term planning into its annual budget.
Some measures under the state ownership policy have been implemented,
but overall progress has been limited. In June 2025 parliament approved the
creation of a unit under the prime minister’s office to maintain a database of SOEs,
oversee SOE financial performance, establish governance guidelines and make
recommendations to the council of ministers for action on specific SOEs. The
government also completed a census of SOEs to facilitate implementation of the
policy. Divestments of major SOEs have stalled, however, despite repeated public
commitments to exit stakes owned by the government and the military. Divestment
proceeds amounted to just US$ 143 million (€125 million) in FY2025, compared
with a budgetary target of US$ 3.4 billion (€3.0 billion), and included the sale of
microfinance institution Tamweely and a minority stake in United Bank.
Green reforms are targeting greater investment in renewable energy. Egypt
agreed a US$ 1.3 billion (€1.1 billion) Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF)
with the IMF in March 2025. Reforms will focus on four areas: accelerating
decarbonisation; analysing and reporting climate-related fiscal risks; strengthening
the management of climate-related data and risks; and increasing financial-sector
climate resilience and identifying the effects of investment plans on national climate
change targets. In July 2025 the Central Bank of Egypt directed banks to support
clients in meeting the European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
requirements. Separately, electricity sector liberalisation is progressing, with the
unbundling of distribution, transmission and generation activities. The Egyptian
Electricity Transmission Company was split from its parent organisation to become
an independent transmission system operator.
